1.2 Osmosis and Water Potential

📖 Definitions
  • Osmosis: Movement of water molecules from a region of higher water potential to a region of lower water potential through a selectively permeable membrane.
  • Water potential (Ψ): The tendency of water to move. Ψ = Ψs + Ψp. Pure water Ψ = 0; all solutions have negative Ψ. Water always moves from higher to lower Ψ.
  • Solute potential (Ψs): Reduction in Ψ due to dissolved solutes. Always negative — more solutes = lower (more negative) Ψs.
  • Pressure potential (Ψp): Hydrostatic pressure. Positive in turgid cells (turgor); negative in xylem under transpiration tension.
  • Why root hairs absorb water: Root hair cell sap contains dissolved ions and sugars → low Ψs → low overall Ψ. Soil water has fewer solutes → higher Ψ. Water moves from soil → root hair by osmosis.

1.3 Water Pathways Through the Root

Apoplastic vs. Symplastic Pathways
The Casparian strip forces ALL water into the symplast before entering the xylem — giving the plant selective control over mineral uptake. Without this gatekeeper, uncontrolled solute entry would disrupt plant water relations.

1.4 Cohesion-Tension Theory (Dixon & Joly, 1894)

Water moves up xylem against gravity because transpiration at leaves creates a negative pressure (tension) in xylem. This pull is transmitted down the continuous water column because of cohesion — the hydrogen bonds between water molecules that prevent the column from breaking.

🔬 Supporting Evidence
  • Stem diameter decreases during the day (xylem under tension shrinks stem) — measured with dendrometers
  • Cutting xylem in air introduces a bubble — water movement stops immediately
  • Pressure bomb experiments (Scholander) confirm xylem sap is under negative pressure
  • Water column in a sealed tube can withstand tensions of up to 30 MPa (cohesive force)

2.2 Stomatal Mechanism — Opening and Closing

Light activates H⁺-ATPase proton pumps in guard cells:

  • Light absorbed by blue-light receptor (phototropin) in guard cells → proton pump activated → H⁺ pumped OUT of guard cells into surrounding epidermis
  • Negative charge inside guard cell attracts K⁺ ions through inward K⁺ channels → K⁺ accumulates inside guard cells
  • Malate²⁻ also accumulates (from starch via PEP carboxylase pathway)
  • Osmotic potential of guard cells drops → water potential decreases → water enters guard cells by osmosis from surrounding cells
  • Guard cells become turgid → the thick inner wall (facing pore) cannot stretch → outer wall bows outward → kidney-bean shape → stoma OPENS

Two main triggers for stomatal closure:

  • Darkness: Photosynthesis stops → CO₂ builds up → pH drops → malate→starch conversion reverses → K⁺ pumped OUT → water potential of guard cells rises → water exits by osmosis → guard cells become flaccid → stoma CLOSES
  • Drought/Water stress: Mesophyll cells lose turgor → abscisic acid (ABA) synthesised and released → ABA binds receptor on guard cell membrane → K⁺ and Cl⁻ efflux channels open → ions leave guard cells → water exits by osmosis → rapid stomatal closure (within minutes)
  • High CO₂ concentration also directly triggers closure — provides feedback when ventilation is poor
🌿

Guard cell uniqueness: Guard cells are the ONLY epidermal cells containing chloroplasts. This allows them to produce ATP for the proton pump, to photosynthesize and reduce CO₂ (triggering opening), and to synthesise malate — all essential to the opening mechanism.

3.2 Importance of Transpiration

+
⬆️
Drives Transpiration Stream
Pulls water and dissolved minerals up through xylem
Transpiration creates the negative pressure (tension) in xylem that drives the continuous upward movement of water and dissolved mineral ions (especially NO₃⁻, H₂PO₄⁻, K⁺) from roots to leaves. Without this, mineral delivery to photosynthesising cells would be severely limited.
+
🌡️
Thermoregulation
Cools leaf by evaporative heat loss
Evaporation of water from mesophyll cells is endothermic — it absorbs heat energy. In tropical Nigeria, leaf temperatures could otherwise exceed 40°C and denature photosynthetic enzymes. Transpiration accounts for 20–50% of heat lost from leaves under high solar radiation.
+
💪
Maintains Turgidity
Keeps non-woody organs firm and functional
Young shoots, leaves, and flower petals rely on cell turgidity for mechanical support. Guard cells must be turgid to open stomata. Turgidity maintains the correct dimensions of cells for metabolic function including organelle positioning and membrane integrity.
+
🧪
Supplies Metabolic Water
Water for photosynthesis and biochemical reactions
Water is a direct reactant in the light reactions of photosynthesis (2H₂O → 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ + O₂). It is also the universal solvent for all enzymatic reactions in the cell. The transpiration stream ensures leaf mesophyll cells continuously receive adequate water for these processes.

6.1 Plants as Primary Producers

Overall equation: 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + light energy → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂

Plants, algae, and cyanobacteria are autotrophs (primary producers) — they convert solar energy and inorganic matter into organic compounds that form the base of virtually all food chains. All consumer organisms (herbivores, carnivores, decomposers) are ultimately dependent on this fixed energy.

🌾

Nigeria's primary productivity: Nigeria is the world's largest cassava producer (~60 million tonnes/year). Understanding cassava photosynthesis — and why maize and sorghum (C4 plants) are more photosynthetically efficient in Nigeria's hot climate than cassava (C3) — is directly relevant to Nigeria's food security and crop improvement research.

6.2 Chloroplast Structure

Chloroplast Ultrastructure — Functions of Each Region
Two distinct functional regions: Thylakoid membranes (light reactions — ATP + NADPH + O₂) and Stroma (Calvin cycle — CO₂ fixation → glucose). This spatial separation allows both processes to run simultaneously without interference.
🌿 Photosynthetic Pigments
  • Chlorophyll a (primary pigment): absorbs red (~680 nm) and blue-violet (~430 nm); reflects green; directly participates in reaction centres (P680 in PSII; P700 in PSI)
  • Chlorophyll b (accessory): absorbs blue and orange-red; passes energy to chlorophyll a; widens the wavelength range usable for photosynthesis
  • Carotenoids (β-carotene — accessory): absorbs blue-violet; reflects orange-yellow; also photoprotective — quenches reactive singlet oxygen to prevent chlorophyll damage
  • Xanthophylls (accessory): absorbs blue; reflects yellow; visible in autumn/dry season when chlorophyll degrades first

9.1 The Calvin Cycle (Light-Independent/Dark Reactions)

Location: Stroma of chloroplast. Key enzyme: RuBisCO (Ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase) — the most abundant enzyme on Earth, and the primary CO₂-fixing enzyme in plants.

Calvin Cycle — Three Stages
The Calvin cycle is a cycle because RuBP is continuously regenerated. For every 3 CO₂ molecules fixed, only 1 net G3P is available for export (equivalent to ½ a glucose molecule). 6 turns are needed to produce 1 net glucose molecule.

10.1 Translocation — Mass Flow Hypothesis (Munch, 1930)

Phloem transports organic assimilates (mainly sucrose, up to 20% w/v) from source (photosynthesising leaves) to sink (growing regions, roots, storage organs, fruits). Unlike xylem (one direction, dead vessels), phloem is bidirectional and uses living sieve tubes.

🔬 Evidence for Phloem Translocation
  • Aphid stylet technique: Aphids tap individual sieve tubes; when the stylet is cut by laser, phloem sap exudes under pressure — confirming positive hydrostatic pressure at source end
  • Ringing experiment: Removing a ring of bark (phloem) stops downward organic transport but NOT upward water flow in xylem — proving phloem is the route for sugars
  • Radioactive labelling: ¹⁴CO₂ fed to source leaf → ¹⁴C-sucrose detected moving in phloem toward sink organs
  • Temperature sensitivity: Cooling a section of stem slows translocation (reduces enzyme activity for active loading) but does not stop xylem flow — phloem is metabolically active

11.2 Anaerobic Respiration — Fermentation in Plants

Equation: C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂ + 2 ATP

Occurs when O₂ is unavailable: in waterlogged soil during Nigeria's rainy season, in compacted soil, and in storage organs under hermetic sealing. Yields only 2 ATP vs. 38 ATP aerobically — plants survive but do not grow well.

📊 Respiratory Quotient (RQ)
  • RQ = CO₂ produced ÷ O₂ consumed
  • Aerobic respiration on carbohydrate: RQ = 6CO₂/6O₂ = 1.0
  • Aerobic respiration on fat (oils in seeds): RQ = <1.0 (e.g. 0.7 for palm oil) — fats need more O₂ per carbon
  • Aerobic respiration on protein: RQ ≈ 0.9
  • Anaerobic/alcoholic fermentation: RQ = ∞ (very large) — CO₂ released, no O₂ consumed
  • RQ > 1.0 in a tissue indicates partial anaerobic fermentation is occurring alongside aerobic respiration
🌿

Compensation point: The light intensity at which the rate of photosynthesis exactly equals the rate of respiration — no net gas exchange occurs. Below the compensation point, respiration exceeds photosynthesis → plant loses organic mass. Above it, the plant gains mass. Shade-adapted plants (e.g. understorey species in Nigerian rainforest) have lower compensation points than sun plants like maize.

12.2 Plant Growth Hormones

+
📐
Auxin (IAA)
Indole-3-acetic acid; produced at shoot apex
At low concentrations: stimulates cell elongation in shoots (promotes H⁺ pump activity → cell wall acidification → loosening → water entry). At high concentrations: INHIBITS growth (especially in roots, which are more sensitive). Controls apical dominance (lateral buds suppressed). Essential for tropic responses. Agricultural use: rooting powders for cassava cuttings; 2,4-D herbicide kills broad-leaved weeds in maize.
+
📏
Gibberellins (GA₃)
Produced in young leaves, seeds, roots
Promote internode elongation (dwarfism due to GA deficiency); break seed dormancy and promote germination; stimulate α-amylase production in germinating cereal grains (important for malting); induce flowering; parthenocarpic fruit development (seedless fruits). Nigerian use: breaking dormancy in stored yam minisetts; used in commercial seedless grape production.
+
✂️
Cytokinins
Produced in root tips, meristems, seeds
Primarily promote cell division (cytokinesis); delay leaf senescence (yellowing — used commercially to keep cut flowers and leafy vegetables fresh longer); promote lateral bud growth (counter to auxin's apical dominance). Essential in plant tissue culture — ratio of cytokinin to auxin determines whether shoot or root develops. Nigerian research use: essential for clonal propagation of elite cassava and plantain varieties.
+
🛑
Abscisic Acid (ABA)
Produced in mature leaves during stress
The stress hormone: triggers stomatal closure during drought (key for water conservation in dry season crops); promotes seed dormancy; inhibits germination; promotes leaf abscission (falling). Important for understanding drought tolerance in Nigeria's savanna region. ABA also protects from cold stress and UV damage.
+
🍌
Ethylene (C₂H₄)
Gaseous hormone; produced by ripening fruits
Promotes fruit ripening (activates enzymes that soften fruit, convert starch to sugar, degrade chlorophyll, produce colour pigments); promotes leaf and fruit abscission (e.g. abscission of ripe mango and plantain in Nigerian markets); promotes root hair formation; inhibits stem and root elongation; stimulates flowering in pineapple. Practical use: ripening bananas emit ethylene → used to ripen other fruits in Nigerian marketplace.

Plant Growth II — Tropisms

Learning Outcomes

  • Explain gravitropism in shoots and roots with reference to different auxin sensitivity
  • Give one Nigerian example for each tropism type

13.1 Definition and Overview

A tropism is a growth movement of a plant organ in response to a directional external stimulus, where the direction of the response is determined by the direction of the stimulus. Tropisms are caused by differential cell elongation on opposite sides of the organ — the result of unequal auxin distribution.

🌱

Key principle: Auxin (IAA) promotes cell elongation in shoots at normal concentrations. The side receiving MORE auxin elongates MORE, causing the organ to CURVE AWAY from that side. Roots are far more sensitive to auxin — concentrations that promote shoot elongation INHIBIT root elongation.

13.2 Tropism Types and Mechanisms

TropismStimulusShoot ResponseRoot ResponseMechanism

13.3 Went's Experiment (1926) — Proof of Auxin in Phototropism

Went's Classic Agar Block Experiment
Went's insight: the chemical that diffused from the coleoptile tip into the agar caused growth when applied to one side. This proved auxin is a DIFFUSIBLE CHEMICAL MESSENGER. The shaded side accumulates more auxin → elongates more → organ bends toward light.

14.1 Taxism (Taxis)

A taxis is the movement of an ENTIRE free-moving organism toward (positive) or away from (negative) a stimulus. Unlike tropism (which is a growth response in a stationary organ), taxism involves locomotion of the whole organism. Taxism only occurs in free-swimming organisms like flagellate algae, bacteria, and motile gametes — NOT in whole rooted plants.

TypeStimulusExample
PhototaxisLightEuglena moves toward moderate light (positive phototaxis) for photosynthesis; moves away from intense light (negative phototaxis) that could damage its chloroplast
ChemotaxisChemical gradientPollen tube grows toward the ovule guided by chemical attractants (calcium ions and γ-aminobutyric acid secreted by the synergids); fern spermatozoids attracted by malic acid from archegonium
ThermotaxisTemperature gradientCertain bacteria move toward warmer temperatures; some motile algae

14.2 Nastism (Nastic Movements)

A nastic movement is a reversible response to a stimulus where the direction of the response is NOT determined by the direction of the stimulus — only by the intensity. Unlike tropisms, nastisms do not result in permanent growth changes; they are usually rapid, reversible, and caused by turgor changes in specialised cells.

+
🌸
Photonasty
Opening/closing in response to light intensity changes
Examples: Flower opening — most flowers open in light and close in dark (e.g. Dandelion, Morning glory — Ipomoea). Leaf folding in Oxalis (Wood sorrel) at night (nyctinasty). These movements are caused by differential turgor changes in specialised pulvinus cells at the base of petals or petioles.
+
🌿
Thigmonasty
Response to touch/mechanical stimulus
Classic example: Mimosa pudica ('touch-me-not' / 'sensitive plant') — widespread weed in Nigerian farmland. On touching, pulvinus cells at the base of leaflets rapidly lose turgor (K⁺ and Cl⁻ efflux → water exits) → leaflets fold together within seconds. This deters herbivores. Recovery takes 10–20 minutes. Also: Venus flytrap (Dionaea) — touch triggers action potential → leaf lobes snap shut in <0.1 second.
+
🌡️
Thermonasty
Response to temperature changes
Example: Tulip flowers open when warmed and close when cooled. The inner surface of petals grows faster in warmth → petals bow outward → flower opens. In cool conditions, outer surface grows faster → petals close inward. Caused by differential growth rates at different temperatures.
