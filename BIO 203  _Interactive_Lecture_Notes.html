MODULE 01

Introduction to Biology, Living Matter & Basic Chemistry

Macromolecules · Characteristics of life · Levels of organization · Properties of water

Learning Outcomes

  • Define biology, biochemistry and physiology and relate them
  • List the eight characteristics that define life
  • Outline the levels of biological organization from atom to biosphere
  • Explain the properties and biological importance of water

1.1 Biochemistry, Physiology & Macromolecules

Biochemistry examines the chemical processes within living organisms, while physiology explores the functions and mechanisms operating within those systems. At the core of life's biochemistry are four major macromolecules — Carbohydrates, Lipids, Proteins and Nucleic Acids — large, complex polymers built from repeating monomers (e.g., amino acids form proteins; nucleotides form nucleic acids).

🧬 Eight Characteristics of Life
  • Cellular organization — all life is made of one or more cells
  • Reproduction — sexual or asexual
  • Metabolism — catabolic and anabolic chemical reactions
  • Homeostasis — a stable internal environment
  • Response to stimuli — reacting to environmental change
  • Growth and development — regulated by genetic instructions
  • Adaptation through evolution — genetic change over generations
  • Heredity — passing DNA/RNA to offspring

1.2 Levels of Organization & Water

Living things are organized hierarchically: Atom → Molecule → Organelle → Cell → Tissue → Organ → Organ System → Organism → Population → Community → Ecosystem → Biosphere. At the molecular level, water's polarity gives it several biologically vital properties: cohesion/surface tension, a high specific heat (buffers temperature change), a high heat of vaporization (enables evaporative cooling — sweating, panting), lower density as ice (so ice floats, insulating aquatic life), and its role as the "universal solvent" for polar and ionic substances.

Property of WaterBiological Importance
High specific heatStabilizes body and ocean temperatures against rapid change
High heat of vaporizationEvaporative cooling — sweating, panting, transpiration
Expands on freezingIce floats, insulating water bodies so aquatic life survives
Universal solventDissolves nutrients, gases, wastes for transport (blood, xylem sap)
Ionization (neutral pH 7)Supports enzyme activity and biochemical stability

1.3 Matter, Atomic Structure & Chemical Bonds

Matter exists as elements (cannot be broken down chemically — O, H, C), compounds (two or more elements chemically bonded in fixed proportions — H₂O, CO₂, NaCl), and mixtures (physically combined, separable — homogeneous like air/saltwater, or heterogeneous like sand in water). Every atom is built from three particles:

ParticleChargeMass (amu)Location
Proton+11Nucleus
Neutron01Nucleus
Electron−1~0Orbitals

The atomic number (Z) = number of protons and defines the element. The mass number (A) = protons + neutrons. Isotopes of an element share the same Z but differ in neutron number and mass (e.g., ¹²C, ¹³C, and radioactive ¹⁴C used in carbon dating). Ions form when atoms gain or lose electrons — cations (positive, e.g., Na⁺) or anions (negative, e.g., Cl⁻) — and are essential for nerve impulses and muscle contraction. Matter exists in four states — solid, liquid, gas, and plasma (a high-energy state found in stars and lightning, where atoms lose electrons).

MODULE 02

Carbohydrates and Their Biological Importance

Mono-, di-, oligo- and polysaccharides · Metabolism · Nutrition

Learning Outcomes

  • Classify carbohydrates by number of sugar units
  • Describe the structure of starch, glycogen, cellulose and chitin
  • Explain the biological importance of carbohydrates
  • Describe carbohydrate metabolism and its hormonal control

2.1 Classification

Carbohydrates are composed of C, H and O generally in a 1:2:1 ratio (CH₂O)ₙ. Monosaccharides (glucose, fructose, galactose) are single-sugar building blocks — glucose, fructose and galactose are structural isomers, all C₆H₁₂O₆. Disaccharides form via dehydration synthesis, releasing water and creating a glycosidic bond: sucrose (glucose+fructose), lactose (glucose+galactose), maltose (glucose+glucose). Oligosaccharides (3–10 units) often sit on cell surfaces for recognition. Polysaccharides are long glucose chains: starch (plant storage, α1-4/α1-6 bonds), glycogen (animal storage, highly branched), cellulose (plant cell walls, β1-4 bonds, indigestible to humans), and chitin (arthropod exoskeletons, fungal walls, nitrogen-containing).

PolysaccharideFound InBond Type
StarchPlant storage (roots, seeds)α 1-4 / α 1-6
GlycogenAnimal liver & muscle storageHighly branched α-glucose
CellulosePlant cell wallsβ 1-4 (indigestible by humans)
ChitinArthropod exoskeleton, fungal wallsNitrogenous modified sugar

2.2 Biological Importance & Metabolism

⚡ Key Roles
  • Energy production — glycolysis + Krebs cycle + ETC can yield up to 38 ATP per glucose
  • Energy storage — starch (plants), glycogen (animals, broken down via glycogenolysis)
  • Structural role — cellulose (plants), chitin (arthropods, fungi)
  • Cell recognition — glycoproteins/glycolipids determine blood types (A, B, AB, O)
  • Brain function — the brain relies almost entirely on glucose; hypoglycemia impairs cognition

Insulin (pancreas) promotes glucose uptake and glycogen storage; glucagon stimulates glycogen breakdown when blood sugar is low. Failure of this regulation causes diabetes (Type 1: insufficient insulin; Type 2: insulin resistance).

2.3 Carbohydrate Metabolism & Nutrition

Glycolysis breaks glucose down in the cytoplasm to pyruvate, ATP and NADH; pyruvate then enters the Krebs cycle and electron transport chain in the mitochondria for further oxidation (fully detailed in Module 8). Carbohydrates supply roughly 45–65% of daily caloric intake in humans. Simple carbohydrates (sugars in fruit, milk, sweeteners) give quick energy but can spike blood sugar; complex carbohydrates (whole grains, vegetables, legumes) digest more slowly for sustained energy. Dietary fibre is also carbohydrate-based: soluble fibre (oats, legumes) helps lower blood cholesterol and glucose; insoluble fibre (whole grains, bran) adds bulk and prevents constipation.

MODULE 03

Lipids and Their Functions in Biology

Fats & oils · Waxes · Phospholipids · Steroids

Learning Outcomes

  • Describe the structure of triglycerides, phospholipids and steroids
  • Distinguish saturated, unsaturated, cis and trans fats
  • Explain the amphipathic nature of phospholipids
  • Explain the biological role of cholesterol

3.1 Fats, Oils & Fatty Acid Types

Lipids are hydrophobic, nonpolar hydrocarbons. A triacylglycerol (triglyceride) forms when three fatty acids esterify to a glycerol backbone (3 ester bonds, releasing 3 H₂O). Saturated fatty acids have only single C–C bonds (e.g., stearic acid) and pack tightly — solid at room temperature. Unsaturated fatty acids have one or more double bonds (e.g., oleic acid) — a cis double bond creates a kink preventing tight packing, so they're liquid (oils) at room temperature. Industrial hydrogenation can convert cis to trans fats (e.g., margarine), which raise LDL ("bad") cholesterol.

TypeBondsState at Room TempExample
SaturatedSingle bonds onlySolidStearic acid, palmitic acid
Unsaturated (cis)≥1 double bond, kinkedLiquid (oil)Oleic acid, olive oil
TransHydrogenated double bondSemi-solidMargarine, shortening

3.2 Phospholipids & Steroids

A phospholipid has two fatty acids + a modified phosphate group on glycerol, making it amphipathic — a hydrophilic head and hydrophobic tails. In water, phospholipids spontaneously form a bilayer (heads out, tails in) — the basis of all cell membranes. Steroids (e.g., cholesterol) have four fused carbon rings; cholesterol is synthesized in the liver and is the precursor of steroid hormones (testosterone, estradiol), vitamin D, and bile salts, and is itself a component of the plasma membrane.

3.3 Waxes & Essential Fatty Acids

Waxes are long fatty acid chains esterified to long-chain alcohols; their hydrophobic nature waterproofs bird feathers and plant leaf surfaces. Essential fatty acids — required by the body but not synthesized by it — must come from the diet. Omega-3 fatty acids (alpha-linolenic acid/ALA, EPA, DHA — found in salmon, trout, tuna) are polyunsaturated with a double bond at the third carbon from the omega end; research links them to reduced heart-attack risk, lower blood triglycerides and blood pressure, anti-inflammatory effects, and inhibited blood clotting. Mammals store fat in adipocytes; plants store oil in seeds as an energy source for seedling development.

Proteins — Structure and Function

Amino acids · The four levels of protein structure · Denaturation

Learning Outcomes

  • Describe amino acid structure and essential vs non-essential amino acids
  • Describe the four levels of protein structure
  • Explain the chemical interactions that stabilize protein shape

4.1 Amino Acids & Protein Roles

Proteins are polymers of amino acids — each has a central (α) carbon bonded to an amino group, a carboxyl group, a hydrogen, and a variable R-group that gives each of the 20 amino acids its chemical identity. Ten are essential (must come from diet: histidine, isoleucine, leucine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan, valine, +arginine in growth). Amino acids join by peptide bonds (dehydration synthesis) to form polypeptides.

Protein TypeExamplesFunction
Digestive enzymesAmylase, lipase, pepsin, trypsinCatabolize nutrients
TransportHaemoglobin, albuminCarry substances in blood
StructuralActin, tubulin, keratinBuild the cytoskeleton
HormonesInsulin, thyroxineCoordinate body systems
DefenseImmunoglobulinsFight pathogens
ContractileActin, myosinMuscle contraction

4.2 Four Levels of Protein Structure

🔬 Structure Hierarchy
  • Primary: the unique amino acid sequence (e.g., insulin's A and B chains)
  • Secondary: local folding — α-helix or β-pleated sheet, from H-bonding along the backbone
  • Tertiary: overall 3-D shape from R-group interactions — hydrophobic interactions, ionic bonds, hydrogen bonds, disulfide linkages (cysteine–cysteine)
  • Quaternary: assembly of multiple polypeptide subunits (e.g., haemoglobin's four chains)

A single amino-acid substitution can be devastating: in sickle-cell anaemia, one substitution in haemoglobin's ~600 amino acids changes both structure and function. Denaturation — loss of shape from heat, pH change, or chemicals — is sometimes reversible (primary structure intact) and sometimes permanent (e.g., a fried egg white).

4.3 Protein Conservation & Amino Acid Chemistry

The chemical nature of each R-group (acidic, basic, polar or nonpolar) determines an amino acid's role within a protein. Some proteins are so functionally critical that their sequence changes very little across evolutionary time — cytochrome c, part of the machinery that harvests energy from glucose, differs by only a handful of amino acids between humans and chimpanzees, more between humans and rhesus monkeys, and by 44 amino acids between humans and yeast — a molecular record of evolutionary relatedness. Proteins may be globular (e.g., haemoglobin) or fibrous (e.g., collagen, keratin) in shape, and their diverse functions include structural support, catalysis (enzymes), transport, defence, movement, storage, and hormonal signalling.

Nucleic Acids — DNA and RNA

Nucleotide structure · The double helix · Types and roles of RNA

Learning Outcomes

  • Describe the structure of a nucleotide
  • Describe the DNA double-helical structure and base pairing
  • Differentiate DNA and RNA

5.1 Nucleotide & DNA Structure

Nucleic acids carry the genetic blueprint of the cell. Each nucleotide has three parts: a nitrogenous base, a pentose (5-carbon) sugar, and a phosphate group. Nucleotides polymerize into DNA or RNA. DNA is double-helical: two antiparallel strands (one 5′→3′, the other 3′→5′) with sugar-phosphate backbones on the outside and nitrogenous bases paired in the middle by hydrogen bonds — Adenine with Thymine, Guanine with Cytosine — like the steps of a spiral staircase. DNA never leaves the nucleus; it uses an RNA intermediary to communicate with the rest of the cell.

FeatureDNARNA
SugarDeoxyriboseRibose
StrandsDouble (helical)Usually single
BasesA, T, G, CA, U, G, C
RoleGenetic storage (stays in nucleus)Protein synthesis intermediary

5.2 The Four Types of RNA & the Central Dogma

A ribonucleotide contains ribose, one of four bases (A, U, G, C) and a phosphate; ribonucleotides link by phosphodiester bonds. There are four major RNA types:

RNA TypeRole
mRNACarries the genetic message copied from DNA to the ribosome
rRNAMajor structural component of ribosomes; its peptidyl transferase activity catalyses peptide bond formation
tRNASmall (70–90 nucleotides); delivers the correct amino acid via anticodon-codon pairing
miRNASmallest type; regulates gene expression by interfering with mRNA messages

Information flows DNA → RNA → protein, known as the Central Dogma of Life: DNA dictates mRNA structure via transcription, and mRNA dictates protein structure via translation. This holds for virtually all organisms, with rare exceptions in some viral infections.

General Enzymology

Enzyme structure · Active site & catalysis · Kinetics · Allosteric regulation

Learning Outcomes

  • Describe the structural levels of enzymes and the role of cofactors
  • Explain how enzymes lower activation energy
  • Discuss factors affecting enzyme activity
  • Explain allosteric regulation and the six enzyme classes

6.1 Enzyme Structure & Catalysis

Enzymes are proteins (structured at primary through quaternary levels) that catalyse reactions by lowering the activation energy needed. The active site binds a specific substrate, forming an enzyme-substrate (ES) complex; many enzymes need non-protein cofactors (metal ions) or organic coenzymes (often vitamin-derived), and some have tightly bound prosthetic groups.

FactorEffect on Enzyme Activity
TemperatureRate rises to an optimum, then falls sharply as the enzyme denatures
pHEach enzyme has an optimum pH; extremes denature it
Substrate concentrationRate rises then plateaus at Vmax once all active sites are saturated
Enzyme concentrationMore enzyme (at fixed substrate) raises rate proportionally
InhibitorsCompetitive (block active site) or non-competitive (bind elsewhere)

6.2 Allosteric Regulation & Enzyme Classes

Allosteric regulation occurs when a regulatory molecule binds a site other than the active site, changing enzyme shape and activity (activation or inhibition) — a key mechanism in metabolic feedback control. Enzymes are classified into six major groups: oxidoreductases (transfer electrons/H), transferases (transfer functional groups), hydrolases (hydrolysis reactions), lyases (break bonds without hydrolysis/oxidation), isomerases (rearrange atoms within a molecule), and ligases (join two molecules using ATP).

6.3 How Enzymes Lower Activation Energy

The active site binds substrate through geometric complementarity (shape fit, like a key in a lock) and chemical complementarity (temporary bonds stabilizing the transition state). Enzymes lower activation energy by several mechanisms: bringing substrates into the correct proximity and orientation; stabilizing the high-energy transition state; providing a favourable microenvironment (e.g., hydrophobic or charged pockets); covalent catalysis (forming a temporary covalent bond with substrate); and acid-base catalysis (donating/accepting protons). The reaction can be summarized: E + S ⇌ ES → EP ⇌ E + P — the enzyme (E) binds substrate (S) to form the ES complex, converts it to the EP (enzyme-product) complex, then releases product (P) unchanged and ready to react again.

6.4 Enzyme Kinetics: The Michaelis-Menten Equation

Enzyme kinetics is described by the Michaelis-Menten equation: v = Vmax[S] / (Km + [S]), where v is reaction velocity, [S] is substrate concentration, Vmax is the maximum rate, and Km (the Michaelis constant) measures the enzyme's affinity for its substrate — a low Km means high affinity. Allosteric enzymes can show cooperativity, where substrate binding at one active site affects activity at other active sites on the same enzyme, allowing fine metabolic control.

Nutrition, Digestion & Absorption

Autotrophic vs heterotrophic nutrition · Feeding types · The human digestive system

Learning Outcomes

  • Distinguish autotrophic and heterotrophic nutrition
  • Classify feeding types and food habits in animals
  • Trace the five stages of nutritional processing
  • Describe the organs of the human digestive system and their enzymes

7.1 Types of Nutrition

Autotrophic nutrition — organisms (green plants, algae) manufacture their own food via photosynthesis. Heterotrophic nutrition — organisms obtain organic nutrients from other organisms; classified by feeding mode: filter feeding (water particles), deposit feeding (soil particles, earthworms), fluid feeding (honey bees), bulk feeding (whole prey, pythons), and ram/suction feeding.

🍽️ Five Stages of Nutrition
  • Ingestion — intake of food through the mouth
  • Digestion — breakdown into absorbable components
  • Absorption — nutrients absorbed, mainly in the small intestine
  • Assimilation — nutrients transported to cells for use
  • Egestion — elimination of undigested waste as feces

7.2 The Digestive System

Food passes: Mouth (chewing, salivary amylase) → Oesophagus (peristalsis) → Stomach (pepsin + HCl, churning) → Small intestine — the major site for both digestion and absorption, receiving bile (fat emulsification) and pancreatic enzymes → Large intestine (water absorption) → rectum/anus.

Digestion is hormonally regulated: gastrin stimulates gastric acid secretion; secretin triggers pancreatic bicarbonate release to neutralize stomach acid in the duodenum; cholecystokinin (CCK) stimulates pancreatic enzyme and gallbladder bile release.

7.4 Malnutrition & Digestive Disorders

⚠️ Malnutrition
  • Kwashiorkor — caused by protein deficiency
  • Marasmus — severe energy (caloric) deficiency
  • Obesity — excess caloric intake; raises risk of Type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease

Common digestive disorders: GERD (stomach acid reflux into the oesophagus), peptic ulcers (from excess acid or H. pylori infection), IBS (functional bowel disorder), and lactose intolerance (lactase deficiency, preventing lactose digestion).

MODULE 08

Respiration, ATP Generation & Cellular Metabolism

Glycolysis · Krebs cycle · Electron transport chain · Fermentation

Learning Outcomes

  • Distinguish aerobic and anaerobic respiration
  • Describe the phases of glycolysis
  • Describe the citric acid (Krebs) cycle
  • Explain the electron transport chain and chemiosmosis

8.1 Overview & Glycolysis

Aerobic respiration requires O₂ and yields far more ATP than anaerobic respiration (fermentation). Glycolysis (cytoplasm) splits glucose into two pyruvate molecules across an energy investment phase (2 ATP used) and an energy payoff phase (4 ATP + 2 NADH produced), net 2 ATP.

8.2 Krebs Cycle & Oxidative Phosphorylation

Pyruvate → acetyl-CoA enters the mitochondrial matrix for the citric acid (Krebs) cycle — a closed loop: acetyl-CoA + oxaloacetate → citrate, then a series of oxidation/decarboxylation steps release CO₂ and generate NADH, FADH₂, and one ATP/GTP per turn, regenerating oxaloacetate. Oxidative phosphorylation (inner mitochondrial membrane) has two parts: the electron transport chain (Complexes I–IV pass electrons from NADH/FADH₂ to O₂, pumping protons into the intermembrane space) and chemiosmosis (protons flow back through ATP synthase, driving ATP formation) — yielding up to 34 ATP. Overall, one glucose molecule can yield up to 38 ATP.

StageLocationNet Yield
GlycolysisCytoplasm2 ATP, 2 NADH, 2 pyruvate
Krebs cycle (×2)Mitochondrial matrix2 ATP, 6 NADH, 2 FADH₂, CO₂
Oxidative phosphorylationInner mitochondrial membraneUp to 34 ATP

When O₂ is scarce, animal cells use lactic acid fermentation (pyruvate → lactic acid), regenerating NAD⁺ to keep glycolysis running.

8.3 Glycolysis Step by Step

Glycolysis has two phases. The energy investment phase uses 2 ATP: (1) hexokinase phosphorylates glucose → glucose-6-phosphate; (2) isomerization to fructose-6-phosphate; (3) phosphofructokinase adds a second phosphate → fructose-1,6-bisphosphate; (4) aldolase cleaves this into G3P and DHAP; (5) DHAP isomerizes to a second G3P — now 2 three-carbon G3P molecules. The energy payoff phase then generates ATP and NADH: (6) G3P is oxidized, reducing NAD⁺ to NADH and forming 1,3-BPG; (7) a phosphate transfers to ADP, forming the first ATP; (8–9) 3-phosphoglycerate rearranges and dehydrates to phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP); (10) a second phosphate transfers to ADP, forming pyruvate and the second ATP.

⚡ Glycolysis Summary
  • Reactants: 1 glucose, 2 ATP invested, 2 NAD⁺, 4 ADP
  • Products: 2 pyruvate, 4 ATP (net gain of 2 ATP), 2 NADH, 2 H⁺, 2 H₂O
  • Regulated mainly at steps 1, 2 (phosphofructokinase) and 7

MODULE 09

Photosynthesis

Pigments · The chloroplast · Light reactions · Calvin cycle · C3/C4 plants

Learning Outcomes

  • Describe photosynthetic pigments and their roles
  • Describe chloroplast structure
  • Outline the light-dependent and light-independent reactions
  • Discuss factors affecting photosynthesis and C3 vs C4 plants

9.1 Pigments & the Chloroplast

Photosynthesis converts light energy into chemical energy stored as glucose, occurring mainly in mesophyll chloroplasts. Chlorophylls absorb mainly red and blue light (reflect green); carotenoids absorb additional wavelengths and protect against photo-oxidation; phycobilins occur in algae/cyanobacteria. Main events: light is absorbed → water is split (photolysis), releasing O₂, electrons and H⁺ → light energy excites electrons → these combine with CO₂ to build sugars.

9.2 Light Reactions, Calvin Cycle & C3/C4

The light-dependent reactions (thylakoid membrane) split water, generate ATP and NADPH, and release O₂. The Calvin cycle (stroma) fixes CO₂ onto RuBP using ATP/NADPH to build G3P, which forms glucose. C3 photosynthesis is the most common pathway (3-carbon first product); C4 plants use a spatial adaptation to concentrate CO₂ and reduce photorespiration in hot, dry climates.

☀️ Factors Affecting Rate
  • Light intensity, CO₂ concentration, and temperature act as limiting factors
  • These factors interact — whichever is scarcest limits the overall rate

9.3 Factors Affecting Photosynthesis & CAM Plants

External factors include light intensity (rate rises then plateaus at the light saturation point), CO₂ concentration, temperature (denatures enzymes if too high, slows them if too low), water availability (drives stomatal opening), light quality (red/blue most effective, green least — reflected by chlorophyll), and oxygen concentration (high O₂ increases competing photorespiration). Internal factors include chlorophyll content, leaf anatomy/thickness, plant age, and nutrient availability (N, Mg, Fe needed for chlorophyll). By the limiting factor principle, the rate of photosynthesis at any moment is capped by whichever factor is closest to its minimum. Besides C3 and C4, CAM (Crassulacean Acid Metabolism) plants (e.g., cacti, succulents) open their stomata at night to fix CO₂, minimizing water loss in arid conditions.

MODULE 10

Protein Synthesis

Replication · Transcription · Translation · The genetic code

Learning Outcomes

  • Distinguish replication, transcription and translation
  • Describe the steps of transcription
  • Describe the steps of translation
  • Explain the roles of the different RNA types

10.1 Three Core Processes

ProcessDescription
ReplicationA double-stranded nucleic acid is duplicated to give identical copies
TranscriptionA DNA gene segment is read and transcribed into a single-stranded mRNA
TranslationThe mRNA sequence is translated into a sequence of amino acids as the protein is built

10.2 Transcription, Translation & the Genetic Code

Transcription (nucleus): RNA polymerase unwinds DNA and synthesizes a complementary mRNA strand from the template strand. Translation (ribosome): mRNA codons (3-base sequences) are read; tRNA molecules bring matching amino acids via complementary anticodons; peptide bonds link amino acids into a growing polypeptide until a stop codon is reached. The genetic code is read in non-overlapping triplets and is largely universal across organisms.

🧬 Roles of RNA
  • mRNA — carries the genetic message from DNA to the ribosome
  • tRNA — delivers specific amino acids, matching anticodon to codon
  • rRNA — structural and catalytic component of the ribosome itself

10.3 The Central Dogma — Nine Possible Information Transfers

The Central Dogma frames how sequence information moves between DNA, RNA and protein — nine conceivable transfers, grouped in three sets. General transfers (normal, occur in most cells): DNA→DNA (replication), DNA→RNA (transcription), RNA→protein (translation). Special transfers (occur in specific contexts): RNA→RNA (RNA replication, some viruses), RNA→DNA (reverse transcription, retroviruses), DNA→protein (rare, no mRNA intermediate). Unknown transfers — protein→protein, protein→RNA, protein→DNA — are not thought to occur naturally, since information flow from protein back to nucleic acid has never been observed. This flow of information is unidirectional and irreversible under normal conditions. In prokaryotes (which lack a nucleus), transcription occurs directly in the cytoplasm rather than a separate nuclear compartment.

Cell Membrane Structure and Function

Phospholipid bilayer · Membrane proteins · Transport mechanisms · Tonicity

  • Describe endocytosis, exocytosis and specialized transport

11.1 Membrane Structure

The membrane is a phospholipid bilayer with embedded/peripheral membrane proteins acting as channels, carriers, receptors and enzymes — giving the "mosaic" nature described by the fluid mosaic model. Small nonpolar molecules cross by simple diffusion (high → low concentration, no energy); water crosses by osmosis (often via aquaporins); larger or charged molecules require facilitated diffusion (carrier proteins) or active transport (ATP-powered, against the gradient, e.g., the sodium-potassium pump).

Solution TypeSolute ConcentrationEffect on Cell
IsotonicEqual to the cell'sNo net water movement
HypotonicLower than the cell'sWater enters — cell may swell/burst
HypertonicHigher than the cell'sWater leaves — cell shrinks (crenation/plasmolysis)

11.2 Bulk Transport & Membrane Functions

Endocytosis brings material into the cell (membrane folds inward, forming a vesicle); exocytosis releases material out. Specialized transporters include ion channels/pumps and aquaporins (rapid water movement). The membrane's key functions: selective permeability, protection, transport, signal transduction (receptors bind ligands to trigger internal responses), and cell-cell communication.

11.3 Transport in Plant vs Animal Cells & Signal Transduction

In plant cells, water moves by osmosis with vacuoles maintaining turgor pressure, and plasmodesmata (channels connecting adjacent cells) allow direct nutrient exchange. In animal cells, nerve cells rely on ion gradients from the Na⁺/K⁺ pump to generate action potentials, while muscle cells use active transport to pump Ca²⁺ back into the smooth ER after contraction. Membrane transport is also central to signal transduction: a signalling molecule (hormone, neurotransmitter) binds a membrane receptor — e.g., a G-protein-coupled receptor — triggering an internal cascade. Ligand-gated ion channels open/close in response to a bound ligand, while other surface receptors trigger changes in gene expression, enzyme activity, or metabolism.

Osmoregulation and Excretion in Animals

Osmoconformers vs osmoregulators · Kidney anatomy · The nephron

  • Distinguish osmoconformers and osmoregulators
  • Describe kidney anatomy and nephron components

12.1 Osmoregulation Strategies

Osmoregulation is the control of water and solute concentration in body fluids to maintain homeostasis — preventing dehydration/overhydration and regulating ions (Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺, Cl⁻, HCO₃⁻). Osmoconformers (mostly marine invertebrates — jellyfish, crabs) have internal osmolarity in equilibrium with their environment. Osmoregulators actively maintain a constant internal osmolarity regardless of external conditions, using different strategies in freshwater, marine or terrestrial habitats.

12.2 Kidney & Nephron Anatomy

Kidney regions: renal capsule (protective outer layer), renal cortex (contains nephrons), renal medulla (renal pyramids with loops of Henle), renal pelvis (collects urine), ureter (to the bladder). Each kidney has ~1–1.5 million nephrons, each with a glomerulus, Bowman's capsule, proximal convoluted tubule, loop of Henle, distal convoluted tubule, and collecting duct.

🩸 Kidney Functions
  • Glomerular filtration — blood pressure forces water/ions/small molecules into Bowman's capsule
  • Tubular reabsorption — glucose, amino acids, water and ions return to blood
  • Tubular secretion — actively removes wastes/excess ions/drugs into the filtrate
  • Countercurrent mechanism (loop of Henle + vasa recta) — concentrates urine
  • Regulates fluid/electrolyte balance, blood pH, blood pressure (via renin), and erythropoiesis (via erythropoietin)

12.3 Habitat Adaptations & Nitrogenous Waste

Freshwater animals (fish, amphibians) live in a hypotonic environment — water constantly enters by osmosis, so they produce large volumes of dilute urine and use gill ionocytes to actively reabsorb Na⁺/Cl⁻. Marine animals face a hypertonic environment and risk dehydration — many drink seawater and excrete excess salt via gill chloride cells, producing concentrated urine. Terrestrial animals combat evaporative water loss with water-conserving kidneys, impermeable skin/cuticles, and behaviours like nocturnality.

Homeostasis and Coordination in Animals

Feedback mechanisms · Nervous system · Endocrine system · Reflex arcs

Learning Outcomes

  • Describe the three components of homeostatic regulation
  • Differentiate negative and positive feedback
  • Describe the divisions of the nervous system and the reflex arc

14.1 Homeostatic Mechanisms

Homeostasis relies on three components: receptors (detect change, e.g., thermoreceptors), control centres (process signals against a "set point," e.g., the hypothalamus), and effectors (carry out the correction, e.g., sweat glands). Negative feedback reverses a deviation (e.g., insulin lowering blood glucose after a meal) and is by far the most common. Positive feedback amplifies a change to rapidly complete a process (e.g., oxytocin intensifying uterine contractions during childbirth).

14.2 Nervous & Endocrine Coordination

The nervous system (CNS: brain + spinal cord; PNS: nerves) provides fast electrical coordination via sensory, integrative and motor functions. A reflex arc — receptor → sensory neuron → integration centre (often spinal cord) → motor neuron → effector — allows rapid, involuntary responses (e.g., withdrawing a hand from a hot object). The endocrine system acts more slowly but with longer-lasting effects, using hormones from glands including the pituitary, thyroid, parathyroids, adrenal glands, pancreas, gonads, pineal and thymus glands.

GlandExample Hormone(s)
PituitaryGrowth hormone, regulates other glands ("master gland")
ThyroidThyroxine — regulates metabolic rate
Adrenal glandsAdrenaline, cortisol — stress response
PancreasInsulin, glucagon — blood glucose control

14.3 Neurons & the Action Potential

The PNS divides into cranial nerves (12 pairs, from the brain) and spinal nerves (31 pairs, from the spinal cord), and functionally into the somatic nervous system (voluntary movement) and the autonomic nervous system — its sympathetic division triggers "fight or flight," while the parasympathetic division restores "rest and digest." A neuron has a cell body (soma), dendrites (receive signals), an axon (transmits signals), and a synapse (junction to the next neuron); types include sensory neurons, motor neurons, and interneurons (connect neurons within the CNS).

⚡ The Action Potential
  • Resting state — interior of the neuron is negatively charged
  • Depolarization — Na⁺ flows in, interior becomes positive
  • Repolarization — K⁺ flows out, restoring negative charge
  • Hyperpolarization — briefly more negative than resting state
  • Return to rest — the Na⁺/K⁺ pump restores the original ion balance

At a synapse, the arriving action potential triggers vesicles to release neurotransmitters into the synaptic cleft; these bind receptors on the next neuron, and are then cleared by re-uptake or enzymatic breakdown.

15.1 Open vs Closed Systems & Heart Evolution

Animal GroupHeartCirculation
Insects/molluscs (open)Simple pump into hemocoelHemolymph bathes organs directly — low pressure
Fish2-chamberedSingle circulation — blood passes through heart once/circuit
Amphibians/reptiles3-chamberedDouble, but O2-rich/poor blood mixes in one ventricle
Birds/mammals4-chamberedDouble, fully separated — most efficient

Blood vessels: arteries carry blood away from the heart (usually oxygenated, except the pulmonary artery); veins return blood to the heart (usually deoxygenated, except the pulmonary vein); capillaries are the thin-walled exchange sites. Pulmonary circulation moves blood between heart and lungs; systemic circulation moves it between heart and body tissues.

15.2 Circulatory Functions & Plant Transport

🩸 Functions of Circulation
  • Transport of gases, nutrients and waste
  • Regulation of body temperature
  • Protection and immunity (white blood cells)
  • Hormonal distribution; fluid and pH/ion balance

Unlike animals, plants have no heart or blood vessels — instead, water and minerals rise through the xylem (driven by transpiration) while manufactured sugars move through the phloem (source-to-sink translocation).

15.3 Xylem & Phloem Mechanisms

Xylem moves water and minerals upward via a combination of transpiration pull (evaporation from leaves creates suction, aided by water's cohesion and adhesion properties), capillary action, and root pressure (an osmotic gradient created by active mineral uptake into roots). Phloem moves sugars by translocation — active transport loads sucrose into the phloem at the "source" (e.g., leaves), raising pressure there; the sap is then pushed along a pressure gradient to a "sink" (roots, fruits, growing buds). Unlike the largely passive xylem stream, phloem transport requires ATP.

PumpHeartNo pump — driven by transpiration pull & pressure gradients

Reproduction in Animals and Plants

Asexual and sexual reproduction in both kingdoms

Learning Outcomes

  • Describe forms of asexual reproduction in animals
  • Describe sexual reproduction and reproductive strategies
  • Describe asexual and sexual reproduction in plants
  • Compare the advantages/disadvantages of each strategy

16.1 Reproduction in Animals

Asexual reproduction produces genetically identical offspring from one parent: binary fission (amoeba, paramecium), budding (hydra, sponges), fragmentation (planaria, some starfish), and parthenogenesis (development of an unfertilized egg — bees, ants, some reptiles). Sexual reproduction involves gamete fusion, producing genetically varied offspring — the basis of most reproductive strategies in complex animals.

16.2 Reproduction in Plants

Asexual (vegetative) reproduction produces clones via: vegetative propagation (runners/stolons, tubers, rhizomes, bulbs), cuttings, layering, and apomixis (seeds formed without fertilization, e.g., some dandelions). Sexual reproduction in plants relies on pollination (wind, water, or animal-mediated) and fertilization, producing genetically varied seeds.

Vegetative StructureExample
Runners/stolonsStrawberries
TubersPotatoes
RhizomesGinger, ferns
BulbsOnions, tulips
⚖️ Asexual vs Sexual — Trade-offs
  • Asexual: fast, preserves desirable traits, but low genetic diversity (vulnerable to disease/change)
  • Sexual: slower, more costly, but generates genetic diversity for adaptation and evolution

16.3 Sexual Reproduction & Strategies

Gametogenesis produces haploid gametes by meiosis — spermatogenesis (testes, 4 sperm per meiosis) and oogenesis (ovaries, typically 1 egg per meiosis). Fertilization may be external (aquatic animals like fish/amphibians, requiring synchronized release of many gametes) or internal (mammals, birds, reptiles). Oviparous animals (birds, reptiles, most fish) lay yolk-nourished eggs; viviparous animals (mammals) give birth to live young nourished via a placenta. Reproductive strategies range from r-strategists (many offspring, little parental care — frogs, fish) to K-strategists (few offspring, heavy parental investment — elephants, humans).

🩸 The Menstrual Cycle (~28 days)
  • Menstrual phase (days 1–5): the endometrium sheds as menstrual bleeding
  • Follicular phase (days 1–13): FSH stimulates follicle growth; estrogen thickens the endometrium
  • Ovulation (~day 14): an LH surge triggers release of a mature egg — the most fertile point
  • Luteal phase (days 15–28): the corpus luteum secretes progesterone to maintain the endometrium; if no fertilization occurs, hormone levels fall and the cycle restarts
